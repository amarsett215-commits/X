## LEVEL 1: THE DISCORD RUG PULL (0:45 – 6:00)

You're 22 years old. You work a job you hate. And you've been watching crypto Twitter long enough to notice something — the people making money aren't the ones buying coins. They're the ones creating them.

So you decide to make your own.

You don't know how to code. That's fine. Nobody checks. You go on Fiverr and pay some developer $150 to fork an existing token contract. You call it something ridiculous — MoonDoge, ElonSafe, whatever is trending this week. The name doesn't matter. What matters is the story.

You spend two days building a website. It looks legitimate enough — a roadmap that goes out to 2028, a team section with AI-generated headshots, a whitepaper you copied from three other projects and remixed with ChatGPT. None of it means anything. But it looks like it does.

Now you need buyers.

You create a Discord server. You airdrop free tokens to 500 wallets to make it look like there's already a community. You join crypto Telegram groups and start posting about your project like you're just a regular holder who's excited.

"Bro have you seen $MOONDOGE? Dev is legit, liquidity is locked, this thing is going to 100x."

That's you. Talking about yourself. In the third person. To strangers.

And it works.

Within 48 hours, you've got 2,000 people in your Discord. They're sharing memes. They're changing their profile pictures. They genuinely believe in a project that took you a weekend to build.

The market cap hits $300,000.

You pull the liquidity.

Every single token becomes worthless in about 11 seconds. Your Discord erupts. People are posting screenshots of their losses. Someone lost their rent money. Someone else is threatening to find you.

You close the Discord. Delete the Telegram. Wipe the website.

You just made $14,000.

You feel two things simultaneously: guilt and the realization that this was the easiest money you've ever made in your life.

Most people would stop here. You don't.

## LEVEL 2: THE SERIAL RUGGER (6:00 – 12:00)

You've done this five times now. Five different tokens. Five different Discord servers. Five communities you built and then destroyed overnight.

But you've gotten smarter.

You don't use your real wallet anymore. You route everything through a chain of wallets — funds go from the liquidity pool to Wallet A, then to Wallet B through a mixer, then to Wallet C on a different chain, then finally to an exchange account registered under a fake ID you bought for $200 on Telegram.

You've also learned that the real money isn't in the pull itself. It's in the buildup.

So now you take your time. You spend three weeks building each project. You hire moderators — real people, not bots — who don't know you're the one running the scam. As far as they know, they're volunteering for a legitimate crypto startup.

You do AMAs on Twitter Spaces. You disguise your voice. You talk about "the vision" and "building in a bear market" and "community-first development." You've learned the vocabulary so well that sometimes you almost convince yourself.

Your projects now hit market caps of $2 to $5 million before you pull.

You're clearing $50,000 to $100,000 per rug.

And you've developed a system. Launch Monday. Build hype for two weeks. Let the chart go parabolic. Pull on a Friday night when the American market is sleeping and the Asian market hasn't woken up. By the time people realize what happened, you're already working on the next one.

You have a spreadsheet tracking your projects. Revenue, expenses, timeline, lessons learned. You review it monthly. You have a rug pull business with quarterly performance reviews.

The guilt? It faded around rug number three.

But here's the thing about Level 2 — you're still small. You're a mosquito. Nobody with real power cares about you yet. The blockchain investigators haven't flagged your wallet patterns. The SEC has bigger fish. The FBI doesn't know you exist.

That's about to change.

## LEVEL 3: THE FAKE EXCHANGE (12:00 – 18:30)

You're done with meme coins. The margins are fine but the ceiling is low and every rug pull leaves a trail of angry people who have nothing to do except try to find you.

So you go bigger. You build a fake crypto exchange.

Not a janky website with a login page. A real, functioning exchange. You hire a development team in Eastern Europe — they don't ask questions, they just build what you spec. The platform looks like Coinbase had a baby with Binance. Clean UI. Two-factor authentication. A working order book.

The trick is the order book is fake.

When users see buy and sell orders flowing across the screen, those are generated by your backend. The prices look real because you're pulling data from actual exchanges and adding a slight delay. Users deposit real crypto. They see their balances go up. They can even make small withdrawals — you process those manually to build trust.

But the money they deposited? It's already in your wallet.

You market the exchange as a "next-gen decentralized platform" targeting users in countries with less regulatory oversight — Southeast Asia, parts of Africa, Eastern Europe. You run Facebook ads. You pay crypto influencers $5,000 each to make videos about it. You sponsor a minor esports team.

Within six months, you have 30,000 registered users and $8 million in deposits.

You keep the exchange running for almost a year. The longer it runs, the more legitimate it looks. Users tell their friends. Some users have six-figure balances — numbers on a screen that represent money you've already moved.

Then one morning, a blockchain analytics firm publishes a report flagging unusual wallet patterns connected to your exchange. A crypto journalist DMs you on Twitter asking for comment.

You have about 72 hours.

You push a "scheduled maintenance" notice on the site. You drain every wallet. You shut down the servers. You send a final email to your entire user base saying the exchange was hacked by North Korean hackers and all funds are lost.

$8 million.

Some of your users file police reports. A class action lawsuit gets organized on Reddit. Your name — your fake name — trends on Crypto Twitter for two days.

And then everyone moves on to the next scandal. That's the beauty of crypto. There's always a next scandal.

But now you have a different problem. $8 million generates attention. The kind of attention that comes with subpoenas and international cooperation agreements and people whose full-time job is tracing blockchain transactions.

You need to disappear. Not metaphorically. Literally.

## LEVEL 4: THE INTERNATIONAL OPERATION (18:30 – 24:00)

You're not in your home country anymore.

You're in a country with no extradition treaty, a flexible banking system, and an immigration office that doesn't ask too many questions if your application comes with a significant "processing fee."

You've bought a second passport. You've grown a beard. You go by a different name. Your friends and family back home think you're working remotely for a tech company in Singapore. In a way, that's almost true.

Because you've scaled up. You're not a solo operator anymore.

You have a team. Eight people across four countries, none of whom have ever met you in person. They know you as a Telegram handle and a voice on encrypted calls. You have:

- Two developers maintaining your infrastructure
- A marketing lead running paid campaigns
- Three "community managers" who interact with victims
- A money laundering specialist who converts crypto to cash through a network of OTC desks and shell companies
- A lawyer in a non-extradition country who structures everything

You're running three scams simultaneously. A yield farming protocol that promises 400% APY. A "play-to-earn" game that requires a $500 NFT buy-in. And a private investment fund for high-net-worth crypto holders who think they're getting exclusive access to pre-sale tokens.

Combined, you're pulling in $2 to $3 million per month.

You live well but not extravagantly — that's a rule. No Lamborghinis on Instagram. No penthouse tours. You've seen what happened to the guys who flexed. They're in prison or on the run. You drive a Toyota. You rent a modest apartment. The $20 million in your cold wallets lives in a safe deposit box in a country you've only visited once.

But you've developed a new problem. Your money launderer is getting sloppy. Two of the shell companies have been flagged by compliance teams. Your developer in Ukraine is drinking too much and posting vague things on social media. And your marketing lead is asking for a bigger cut — phrasing it in a way that sounds a lot like a threat.

This is the part nobody tells you about running a criminal enterprise. It's not the law that gets you. It's the people.

The people always become the problem.

## LEVEL 5: THE APEX — THE GHOST (24:00 – 29:00)

You've been doing this for four years. Your total take is somewhere north of $60 million. You've laundered most of it through real estate purchases in three countries, a chain of laundromats that actually wash clothes, and a legitimate crypto investment fund that you seeded with dirty money and now operates legally.

You are, on paper, a successful entrepreneur.

You attend blockchain conferences under your new name. You shake hands with regulators. You've donated to a children's hospital. Last month, you were on a panel about "building trust in the crypto ecosystem."

Nobody in that room had any idea.

But here's what life actually looks like at Level 5.

You haven't spoken to your family in two years. Not because you can't — because you're afraid their phones are monitored. Your mother thinks you're dead or that you don't care. You can't explain the truth because the truth would make her an accessory.

You have no real friends. Everyone in your life either works for you and doesn't know who you are, or knows who you are and could destroy you. Trust is a concept that no longer applies to your existence.

You change phones every two weeks. You use a VPN routed through three countries to check your email. You never connect to Wi-Fi in your apartment — you use a mobile hotspot registered to a name that doesn't exist. You pay for everything in cash. You keep a go-bag by the door with three passports, $50,000 in mixed currencies, and a burner laptop with nothing on it.

You've rehearsed your escape route. If someone knocks on your door at 3 AM, you can be in a different country in six hours.

And every single night, you check the same three websites. The FBI's press releases. Interpol's public notices. And a blockchain analytics dashboard that monitors every wallet you've ever touched.

Because the thing about crypto fraud at this level is that the blockchain never forgets. Every transaction you've ever made is permanently recorded on a public ledger. The technology you exploited to steal $60 million is the same technology that keeps a perfect, immutable record of every dollar you took.

You didn't beat the system. You're just ahead of it. For now.

Somewhere in an office in Washington, or The Hague, or Singapore, there's an analyst looking at a cluster of wallets that all trace back to the same pattern. Your pattern. They don't know your name yet. They don't know what country you're in. But they have the one thing that doesn't expire.

The blockchain.

And it's patient.
